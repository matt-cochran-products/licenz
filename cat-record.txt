1. Hardening the Local State
Since you are using Rust, you have a few ways to make this tamper-resistant:
Sealed Storage: Don't just save a JSON file. Use a crate like Age or Ring to encrypt the state with a key derived from your hardware fingerprint. This ensures that even if a user copies the "time state" to a different machine, it’s unreadable.
Hidden Files vs. Registry: On Windows, scattering bits of the timestamp in the Windows Registry or as Alternative Data Streams (ADS) makes it significantly harder for a non-technical user to "reset" your clock.
2. Handling "False Positives"
The biggest risk with time-tracking in airgapped systems (especially Industrial/IoT) is CMOS battery failure. If a machine loses power and the BIOS resets to 1970/2000, your software will look at your encrypted "2024" timestamp and permanently lock the user out.
The "Grace Margin": Most professional tools allow a small negative drift (e.g., 1 hour) to account for timezone changes or minor clock sync issues.
The Admin Unlock: You’ll need a Challenge-Response mechanism in your CLI. The user sends you a "Lock Code," and your SaaS generates a one-time "Time Correction" key to reset the local state.
3. The SaaS Integration
Since your backend is "Stripe-like," you can track the "Last Reported Time" whenever they do a Sneakernet update.
If a user tries to activate a new license file but their uploaded request shows an older timestamp than their previous one, your SaaS can flag it as a Clock-Tampering Anomaly before ever issuing the new license.
